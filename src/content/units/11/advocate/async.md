# Unit 11: Deployment & Going Live — Async Materials
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 30–45 min

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between a soft launch and a public launch and articulate why the sequencing matters for institutional credibility
- Draft a one-paragraph usage policy that sets honest expectations for researchers using the archive
- Identify the three communications audiences for a launch and the key message for each

## Reading

### What "Going Live" Means Institutionally

From a technical standpoint, "going live" is a 10-minute operation: a developer pushes a button, the archive is deployed to a public URL, and it's accessible worldwide. From an institutional standpoint, going live is a much bigger event — and the institutional preparation is what the advocate is responsible for.

Advocates need to think about going live in three dimensions: access, communications, and sustainability commitments.

**Access policy:** Who is allowed to use the archive? At launch, you have three main options:
- **Open public access:** Anyone with the URL can use it. This is the default for web-deployed archives and is appropriate for archives of historical public figures in the public domain.
- **Restricted access:** The URL is shared only with a specific audience (a seminar, a research group) during a soft launch period.
- **Authenticated access:** Users log in before they can query the archive. This adds friction but allows usage tracking by individual researcher.

For most scholarly archives of public-domain historical figures, open public access is the right answer. The archive's purpose is broad dissemination of historical knowledge. Restricting access contradicts that purpose.

**Usage policy:** Even for open-access archives, a brief usage policy sets appropriate expectations. A good usage policy for a living archive covers:
- What the archive can and cannot answer (scope of the corpus)
- That answers are AI-generated synthesis and must be verified against primary sources
- How to cite the archive in scholarly work
- Contact information for corrections or additions

This isn't a legal document. It's a paragraph or two on the About page that helps researchers use the archive well. It should acknowledge limitations honestly — an archive that overpromises and underdelivers damages institutional credibility more than one that sets clear expectations and meets them.

### The Two-Launch Model: Soft Launch then Public Launch

The most effective go-live strategy is a two-phase launch:

**Soft launch (Weeks 1–4):** Share the URL with a small, trusted group — expert scholars in the figure's field, librarians familiar with the corpus, graduate students who will use the archive in their research. Ask them explicitly: "What did the archive get right? What did it get wrong? What questions can't it answer that it should be able to?" Incorporate their feedback before the public launch.

The soft launch serves two institutional purposes:
1. It surfaces quality issues before the general public (and the press) sees them
2. It creates a group of informed advocates who can speak to the archive's value from firsthand experience

**Public launch (Month 2+):** The archive is ready for general use. Communications go out. Press contacts are notified. The archive has been stress-tested by people who know the corpus well enough to catch errors.

This sequencing also gives the technical team time to observe real usage patterns and make adjustments — to the system prompt, the UI, the corpus — before traffic ramps up.

### The Three Launch Communications Audiences

A public launch typically requires three separate communications, each with a different message:

**1. Scholarly community:** The archive is a research infrastructure investment. Message: "A new tool for Du Bois scholarship, built on [corpus description], returns [quality benchmark] on expert test questions." Lead with scholarly rigor, not novelty.

**2. Institutional stakeholders (administrators, funders):** The archive demonstrates the institution's commitment to digital humanities and public access. Message: "Our institution has built infrastructure that serves researchers, educators, and the public. Year 1 sustainability is secured by [X]. We are seeking partners for Year 3+."

**3. General public and press:** The archive makes historical scholarship accessible. Message: "You can now ask Du Bois your questions directly — with citations." Lead with the experience, not the technology.

### Year-1 Sustainability Commitments

Before any public launch communications go out, the following commitments should be in writing:

1. Which person or role is the "archive steward" — responsible for monitoring quality and corpus updates?
2. Which institutional budget covers operating costs for Year 1?
3. What is the process for taking the archive offline if a critical quality issue is discovered?
4. Who reviews and approves additions to the corpus?

These aren't bureaucratic formalities — they're the difference between an archive that thrives for five years and one that silently degrades and is quietly forgotten.

## Reflection Questions
1. A journalist contacts your institution asking to write a profile of the archive before your planned public launch date. How do you respond? What conditions, if any, do you set?
2. Write a two-sentence usage policy for your archive. Include one honest statement about its limitations.
3. Your soft launch reveals that the archive confidently gives wrong answers to three categories of questions. The public launch is scheduled for three weeks from now. What do you do?

## Prep Task
Before your next session: Draft a soft launch email to 5–10 expert scholars or practitioners who know your figure's work. The email should explain: what the archive is, what you're asking them to test, how to submit feedback, and when you plan the public launch. Bring the draft to your next session.
