# Unit 11: Deployment & Going Live — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Slides, sample soft launch email template (shared in chat), usage policy examples

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: share your soft launch email draft — what was hard to write? |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: the institutional launch framework |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: launch planning workshop |
| 1:00–1:15 | Full group debrief + Year-1 commitment checklist review |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: What Goes Live Is Not Just the URL**
- Technical deployment: 10 minutes
- Institutional readiness: the advocate's domain
- Four things that must exist before public launch: access policy, usage policy, steward named, sustainability commitment for Year 1
- Frame: you're launching a service, not releasing code

**Slide 2: The Two-Launch Model**
- Soft launch: trusted audience, 4 weeks, active feedback collection
- Purpose: surface quality issues before the press sees them; build your first cohort of advocates
- Public launch: general access, communications go out to all three audiences
- The risk of skipping soft launch: a viral bad answer destroys institutional credibility

**Slide 3: Three Audiences, Three Messages**
- Scholarly community: research infrastructure, citation quality, corpus scope
- Institutional stakeholders: institutional investment, Year-1 sustainability secured, partnership opportunities for Year 3+
- General public/press: accessible scholarship, conversational interface, "ask Du Bois your questions"
- Show example first sentences for each message type

**Slide 4: Communicating Limitations Honestly**
- Why honesty is strategically smart, not just ethically right: overpromising and underdelivering damages future funding opportunities
- The "hallucination" question — how do you answer when a journalist asks "can it make things up?"
- Honest answer: "The archive can only answer from the corpus. It can misrepresent what a passage says, but it cannot invent passages. Every answer includes a citation you can check."
- Include limitations on the About page; this is not an admission of failure, it's a mark of scholarly integrity

**Slide 5: The Year-1 Commitment Checklist**
- Show the four institutional commitments required before public launch
- Discussion: which of these is hardest to get in writing at your institution?
- Who has the authority to make each commitment? (named person, not role)

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 3–4**

Each group works through the launch planning framework for one hypothetical or real archive:

1. Choose your launch date (be specific — a month and year, not "when it's ready")
2. Define the soft launch audience: who are your 5–10 expert testers?
3. Draft the first paragraph of each of the three launch communications (scholarly, institutional, public)
4. Identify the one commitment that will be hardest to get in writing — and name who you'd go to first

Groups report back with: their launch date, their soft launch audience, and the hardest commitment to get in writing.

## Debrief Questions
1. What made writing the "honest limitations" section of the usage policy feel uncomfortable? What would make it easier to do well?
2. If your soft launch reveals serious quality issues and you need to delay the public launch by two months, how do you communicate this to institutional stakeholders who were expecting the original date?
3. The archive goes live. A month later, you have no usage data because you didn't set up analytics. What have you lost, and how do you make the case for Year 2 funding without it?

## Facilitator Notes
- The "communicating limitations honestly" slide often generates the richest discussion. Many advocates are anxious about acknowledging AI limitations in public communications, fearing it will undermine credibility. The counter-argument — that honest framing of limitations is a mark of scholarly integrity that builds long-term trust — is worth spending time on.
- The "who has authority" question in the checklist slide is deliberately pointed. Many institutions have diffuse authority for digital projects. Identifying a specific named person (not "the provost's office") is the difference between a real commitment and a bureaucratic gesture.
- For participants without a specific archive in mind: the breakout exercise works fine with a hypothetical. Have them choose a historical figure and work through the launch framework as if the archive were 90% complete.
- Remind participants: the advocate's job doesn't end at launch. Year 2 is when most archives fail — grant ends, steward moves on, corpus becomes stale. The Year-1 commitment checklist is the foundation of Year 2 survival.
