# Unit 11: Deployment & Going Live — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Tables of 4, projection screen, whiteboard
**Materials:** Printed launch planning worksheets (one per participant), Year-1 commitment checklist cards

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: the "day before launch" scenario |
| 0:10–0:30 | Presentation: institutional launch framework |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: launch planning across three audiences |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share + peer review of soft launch emails |

## Warm-Up Activity (10 min)
Post this on the board:

> It's the day before your archive's planned public launch. A colleague on the soft launch team emails: "I asked it three questions I know the answer to, and it got two of them confidently wrong. What should we do?"

Ask participants: What do you do? Take responses and note the range — from "delay the launch" to "add a disclaimer" to "fix the prompts overnight." Don't resolve the tension yet; return to it after the presentation.

## Facilitated Presentation (20 min)

### The Three Institutional Readiness Criteria

Before any public launch communications go out, three things must be true:

**1. The archive has been tested by people who know the corpus.**
This is the soft launch. "Testing" doesn't mean clicking around — it means expert scholars asking questions they know the answers to and evaluating the accuracy of the responses.

**2. An honest usage policy is live on the About page.**
This includes: what the archive can and cannot answer, how to cite it, and who to contact with corrections. Write it now, before launch, while you're still in control of the framing.

**3. A named institutional steward is committed to Year 1.**
Not a role. A person. With a budget line. Who has agreed in writing.

### Communicating Limitations

Return to the warm-up scenario: two wrong answers out of three expert questions. This is not a launch-blocker in itself — it depends on the type of questions, the nature of the errors, and whether they're fixable. But the scenario illustrates why the soft launch exists.

On the whiteboard, draw two columns: "Delay and fix" vs. "Launch with caveats." Work through:
- If the errors are systematic (all from the same corpus gap): delay and fix
- If the errors are edge cases (obscure biographical details, not core interpretive questions): launch with honest caveats in the usage policy
- If the errors are in the most likely search queries: delay

The decision framework is: *Would an expert user encounter this error in normal use?* If yes, fix it first. If only a specialist would notice, caveat it.

### The Three-Audience Communications Framework

Walk through the three audiences and their messages. Show example opening sentences:
- Scholarly: "The [Figure] Living Archive is a research tool that retrieves and synthesizes passages from [N] texts in [Figure's] public-domain corpus..."
- Institutional: "After [N] months of development, [Institution] is proud to launch..."
- Public: "Now you can ask [Figure] your questions. Answers come with citations..."

Ask: What does each audience want to know first? What concerns do they have?

## Workshop Activity (30 min)

### Round 1: Draft Your Three Opening Sentences (10 min)
Each participant works individually using the printed worksheet. Write one opening sentence for each audience:
- Scholarly community email/blog post
- Institutional stakeholder update
- General public announcement or social media post

The sentences will feel different. That's correct. The archive is the same, but the frame changes with the audience.

### Round 2: Draft the Usage Policy (10 min)
Using the worksheet, write a 3-sentence usage policy for your archive:
- Sentence 1: What the archive is and what corpus it draws from
- Sentence 2: How to interpret and verify AI-generated answers
- Sentence 3: How to cite the archive; who to contact with corrections

Share with the person next to you. Does it set honest expectations? Does it discourage appropriate use?

### Round 3: The Year-1 Commitment Checklist (10 min)
Using the Year-1 commitment checklist card, identify for your archive:
- Who is the named steward?
- Which budget line covers Year 1 operating costs?
- What is the takedown procedure if a critical quality issue is found?
- Who approves corpus additions?

Any blank line is a pre-launch task.

## Gallery Share (15 min)
Participants post their three opening sentences on the wall, grouped by audience type (scholarly / institutional / public).

Facilitator walks the gallery:
- In the scholarly column: which sentences lead with research infrastructure? Which lead with access? What does the difference signal about institutional priority?
- In the public column: which sentences lead with the experience? Which lead with the technology? (Rule: lead with experience.)
- Note patterns: Are any sentences identical across audiences? (This means someone wrote one message for everyone — a common mistake.)

Return to the warm-up scenario and close: "You now have the framework to make that delay-or-launch decision — not based on anxiety, but based on a clear set of questions."

## Facilitator Notes
- The warm-up scenario is the most important moment in this session. It creates emotional investment in the launch framework by making the stakes real before the framework is introduced.
- During Round 2 (usage policy), watch for overly legalistic language. Some participants will draft a disclaimer that sounds like a terms-of-service agreement. Redirect: "Would a historian read this and feel confident using the archive? Or would they close the tab?"
- The Year-1 commitment checklist will have blank lines for most participants. This is a feature, not a bug — it generates a specific action list for after the session.
- The gallery share comparison (which sentences differ across audiences vs. which are identical) is most effective if the facilitator can point to specific examples. Be prepared to highlight 2–3 contrasts.
