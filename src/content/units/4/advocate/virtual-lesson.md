# Unit 4: Text Quality & Preparation — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Slides, shared quality audit template spreadsheet, sample text files (3 quality levels, pre-prepared by facilitator)

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: share one finding from your quality audit prep task |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: quality audit methodology + remediation scoping |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: build a tiered quality decision list for your corpus |
| 1:00–1:15 | Debrief: how will you communicate quality decisions to your team? |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: Quality as a Curatorial Responsibility**
- The archive treats all passages equally — it can't tell good OCR from bad
- Every quality-1 text you include introduces noise that degrades the entire archive's precision
- This is not a technical decision deferred to the developer — it is a curatorial decision
- The Advocate's job: define the quality standard, conduct the audit, make the inclusion decisions
- "Include everything and let the AI sort it out" is not a strategy — it is an abdication

**Slide 2: The Quality Audit Methodology**
- Random sample: 10–15% of files, not cherry-picked
- Read 3–5 paragraphs from the middle of each file (not the beginning)
- The 1–2–3 scale: what each level looks like in practice
- Live demo: read 3 sample texts aloud, class rates them before facilitator reveals the rating
- The reportable metric: "X% at quality 2 or above, assessed by [person] on [date]"

**Slide 3: The Four-Tier Decision Framework**
- Tier 1: Include as-is (quality 2–3, covers important ground)
- Tier 2: Include with minor cleanup (quality 2 with specific fixable errors — proper name normalization, obvious OCR artifacts)
- Tier 3: Attempt remediation or find alternative (quality 1 but high scholarly value — only text of its kind)
- Tier 4: Exclude (quality 1, covers ground already in corpus, or marginal scholarly value)
- The framework is a communication tool — it turns subjective judgment into actionable categories

**Slide 4: Scoping Remediation Work**
- What remediation involves: manual correction, OCR re-run, or finding a better source copy
- Time estimates: quality 2 cleanup — 30 min per text; quality 1 re-run — 2–4 hours per text; quality 1 manual — days
- The break-even question: is this text's unique scholarly contribution worth the remediation time?
- What to tell your technical team: tiered list, not a vague request to "clean up the corpus"
- Budget implication: remediation can be a line item in grant proposals

**Slide 5: Communicating Quality Standards in Writing**
- Concrete standards that can be implemented: "quality 2 or above on the 1–3 scale"
- Not: "good enough" or "readable" or "usable"
- The written standard creates accountability — reference it when quality issues arise post-launch
- Include in your project documentation: the quality standard, the audit date, the auditor, the results
- This documentation protects your institution if a scholar questions a source

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 3–4**

Each group works with one participant's quality audit results from the prep task. Using the four-tier framework:

1. Assign each audited text to a tier (10 min) — work through the list together; push back on tier assignments that seem too lenient or too conservative
2. For any Tier 3 texts: what specifically would remediation involve? How long would it take? Who would do it? (10 min)
3. Draft a one-paragraph quality standards statement for the project: "All texts in [figure]'s archive meet the following quality standard: ___" (5 min)

Groups come back with: a tiered list for the audited texts + a draft quality standards statement.

## Debrief Questions
1. Did any group disagree sharply about a Tier 3 text — one that some wanted to include and others wanted to exclude? What was the deciding factor?
2. How did the draft quality standards statements differ across groups? Was there consensus on what "quality 2" means in plain language?
3. What's one thing you'd communicate differently to your technical team after this session?

## Facilitator Notes
- Some participants will resist the four-tier framework as oversimplified — push them to articulate the criteria they'd use instead; usually they arrive at something equivalent
- The "Tier 3" category is where the most important conversations happen — genuinely unique texts with poor OCR quality require the most judgment
- Facilitators should prepare real example texts at each quality level from the Du Bois corpus or another public-domain figure — abstract descriptions of quality levels are less effective than hearing actual quality-1 text read aloud
- The quality standards statement exercise often produces surprisingly varied language — sharing drafts across groups is instructive
