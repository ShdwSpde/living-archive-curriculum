# Unit 2: Choosing a Historical Figure — Async Materials
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 30–45 min

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the qualities that make a historical figure well-suited for a living archive
- Research a figure's corpus using public digital collections
- Frame an archive proposal in institutional terms — scholarly significance, audience, and stewardship

## Reading

### What Makes a Figure Well-Suited?

Not every historical figure is an equal candidate for a living archive. The ideal candidate has a large body of **public-domain written work** available as digitized text. The more they wrote — and the more of it has been digitized — the richer the archive.

**The public domain threshold:** In the United States, works published before 1931 are in the public domain as of 2026. This window advances one year each January. A figure who was prolific through the 1920s has a usable corpus today; a figure whose major works appeared in the 1930s or later requires careful copyright review.

**What makes a corpus rich:**
- Books and monographs (dense, well-structured, strong for interpretive questions)
- Edited periodicals (high volume, good for tracing the evolution of ideas)
- Speeches and essays (shorter form, good for comparative questions)
- Letters and correspondence (intimate, but often sparsely digitized)

The Du Bois archive benefits from all four: major books, 218 issues of *The Crisis*, hundreds of essays, and significant correspondence — nearly all pre-1931 and digitized on Internet Archive.

### Figures Well-Suited for This Approach

**Frederick Douglass (1845–1895):** 5 books + hundreds of speeches + *The North Star* / *Frederick Douglass' Paper*. Library of Congress digital collections.

**Ida B. Wells (1892–1920s):** 3 books + pamphlets + *Free Speech* columns. Project Gutenberg, University of Chicago digital archives.

**Booker T. Washington (1895–1915):** 8+ books + speeches + *The Tuskegee Student*. Internet Archive, Tuskegee University archives.

**Anna Julia Cooper (1892–1925):** *A Voice from the South* + essays + speeches. Internet Archive, Howard University.

**Carter G. Woodson (1915–1928):** 5+ books + *Journal of Negro History* articles. JSTOR (public domain issues), Internet Archive.

**Marcus Garvey (1914–1927):** Speeches + *The Negro World* editorials + essays. UCLA Garvey Papers, Internet Archive.

### Making the Institutional Case

An archive isn't just a tech project — it's a curatorial argument. Before proposing an archive, you need to articulate:
- **Why this figure, at this institution?** What is the scholarly or community stake?
- **Who are the users?** Students, researchers, journalists, general public, descendant communities?
- **What questions will they bring?** A figure's archive should answer questions people actually have — not just questions a scholar imagines.
- **What's the gap?** How does a living archive complement existing digital collections, print bibliographies, or archival finding aids?

## Reflection Questions
1. Think about your institution's mission and community. Which historical figure would be most meaningful to archive — and why would your institution be the right steward?
2. Look up your candidate figure on Internet Archive (archive.org). How many text files can you find? Are they .txt or .pdf? What years do they cover?
3. Write one sentence describing the archive's purpose: "This archive lets [users] ask [type of questions] about [figure]'s [specific body of work]."

## Prep Task
Before your next session: Search Internet Archive for your candidate figure. Find at least 3 texts you could include in a corpus. Note the file formats available, the publication dates, and whether the OCR quality looks reasonable (open a .txt file and read a paragraph — does it make sense?).
