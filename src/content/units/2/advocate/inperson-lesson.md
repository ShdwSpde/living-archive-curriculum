# Unit 2: Choosing a Historical Figure — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Tables arranged for small groups of 3–4; projector or large screen at front; whiteboard or chart paper for sticky-note gallery
**Materials:** Sticky notes (two colors), markers, printed one-page "Figure Evaluation Rubric" handout, laptop per group or shared device with Internet Archive open

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: "Name a figure, name a question" |
| 0:10–0:30 | Facilitated presentation: corpus, copyright, institutional case |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: figure evaluation stations |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share: purpose statements on the wall |

## Warm-Up Activity

**"Name a figure, name a question" (10 min)**

Each participant writes on a sticky note:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** The historical figure they're considering for their archive
- **Color 2 (pink):** One question they'd want the archive to answer

Post both stickies on the whiteboard. Facilitator does a quick read-aloud scan: notice how many figures have similar dates, how many questions are interpretive vs. factual, whether any figures appear multiple times. This takes 3–4 minutes and sets up the criteria presentation naturally — you can point back to the stickies throughout.

## Facilitated Presentation

**Three criteria for a well-suited figure (20 min)**

**1. Corpus size and format (7 min)**
A living archive is only as good as its corpus. Walkthrough:
- What does "public domain" mean in practice: works published before 1931 are usable as of 2026
- The spectrum of source types: books (dense, great for interpretive questions), periodicals (high volume, good for tracing ideas over time), speeches and essays (shorter, good for comparison), letters (intimate but often sparse)
- Why format matters: .txt files are ideal, _djvu.txt files are usable, PDFs are difficult to work with cleanly
- Live demonstration: pull up one of the figures from the sticky notes on Internet Archive, count texts, open a .txt file and read one paragraph aloud — ask the room: "Does this OCR quality seem workable?"

**2. The public domain map (6 min)**
Draw a timeline on the board from 1900 to 1940. Mark 1931 as the current cutoff. Walk through a split-corpus scenario: a figure active from 1915–1945. What's usable? What requires copyright review? Key point: government documents have no date cutoff (always public domain). Periodicals must be checked issue by issue. Figures who published heavily in the 1920s are the sweet spot.

**3. The institutional stake (7 min)**
The hardest criterion to articulate, but often the most important for getting institutional support. Ask: "If you already have an Internet Archive search and a library catalog — what does a living archive add?" The answer is interpretive depth and conversational access. Push participants to name specific user types at their institution: a first-generation undergrad writing a history paper, a community organization documenting local history, a journalist researching a piece. Specific users make stronger institutional arguments than vague ones.

## Workshop Activity

**Figure evaluation stations (30 min)**

Three stations around the room. Groups rotate every 10 minutes (or stay at one station if time is short and groups are large):

**Station 1 — Corpus check**
Laptop open to Internet Archive. Each group member searches for their figure or a shared practice figure. Task: find at least 5 texts, note file formats, open 2–3 .txt files and rate OCR quality 1–3 (1=poor/garbled, 2=readable with issues, 3=clean). Record results on the station worksheet.

**Station 2 — Copyright map**
Printed timeline handout with blank boxes. Each person plots their figure's major works by publication year. Color-code: green = pre-1931 (usable), yellow = 1931–1978 (requires research), red = post-1978 (almost certainly under copyright). At the end, each person should be able to say: "X% of my figure's major works are clearly in the public domain."

**Station 3 — Purpose statement drafting**
Prompt on chart paper: "This archive lets [users] ask [questions] about [figure]'s [specific corpus]." Each participant drafts one sentence. Group reads drafts aloud and gives feedback: Is the user specific? Is the question type specific? Is the corpus named? Revise once.

## Synthesis / Gallery Share

**Purpose statements on the wall (15 min)**

Each participant writes their revised purpose statement on a large sticky note and posts it on the wall in three columns: "Users," "Questions," "Corpus." Facilitator leads a 5-minute read-through, pointing out strong examples of specificity and any common gaps. Remaining 10 minutes: open conversation — which figures generated the most interesting purpose statements? Which had the thinnest corpus? What's one thing participants would research before the next session?

Close by naming the prep task: before the next session, go back to Internet Archive and download one .txt file from your figure's corpus. Open it in a text editor and read 2 pages. Come ready to describe what you found.

## Facilitator Notes
- If participants don't have a figure in mind yet, have 3–4 practice figures prepared (Ida B. Wells, Carter G. Woodson, Anna Julia Cooper, Booker T. Washington) — they can evaluate a practice figure while forming their own ideas
- Station 2 (copyright map) moves fastest — if groups finish early, have them research one ambiguous case (e.g., a work published without a date)
- The purpose statement gallery is where most learning consolidates — don't cut it short; if the workshop runs long, compress the station rotation instead
- Watch for purpose statements that are too broad ("this archive makes history accessible") — the test is whether someone could design a very different archive from the same statement; if yes, it needs more specificity
