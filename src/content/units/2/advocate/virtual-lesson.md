# Unit 2: Choosing a Historical Figure — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Slides, Internet Archive browser tab, note-taking doc

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: what figure are you considering and why? |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: Corpus evaluation + institutional framing |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: peer feedback on figure proposals |
| 1:00–1:15 | Full group: refining the institutional case |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: The Three Criteria**
- Large body of public-domain written work (the raw material)
- Questions scholars and communities actually want to ask (the demand)
- Institutional stake — why your organization, why now (the argument)
- All three must be true. One weak criterion doesn't disqualify, but two do.

**Slide 2: Reading a Corpus on Internet Archive**
- Live demo: search "[figure name] site:archive.org"
- Identify .txt vs. _djvu.txt vs. .pdf — txt is best, djvu is usable, pdf is hard
- Spot-check OCR quality: open a .txt file, read a paragraph
- Count texts: aim for 15–50 primary sources minimum

**Slide 3: The Public Domain Map**
- Draw a timeline: 1931 cutoff as of 2026 (advances annually)
- Show a split corpus example: figure active 1920–1960 — what's usable?
- Government publications: always public domain, no date cutoff
- Periodicals: check each issue's publication date individually

**Slide 4: Making the Institutional Case**
- Who at your institution would use this archive? Name them specifically.
- What existing collection does it extend or complement?
- What's your institution's stake — research mission, community connection, collections strategy?
- One slide ≠ one paragraph. Each bullet should take 60 seconds to say.

**Slide 5: The Purpose Statement Formula**
- Template: "This archive lets [users] ask [questions] about [figure]'s [corpus]."
- Good: "This archive lets scholars and students ask interpretive questions about Frederick Douglass's evolving views on racial uplift across his three autobiographies and newspaper editorials."
- Bad: "This archive will make Du Bois's work accessible to people."

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 3–4**

Each person shares their figure candidate (2 min). Group spends 5 min per person:
1. Is the corpus large enough? What's your evidence?
2. Is the public domain situation clear? Any risk areas?
3. Who at your institution would use this — can you name 3 specific people or roles?
4. What's one competing resource (existing collection, database) — and why isn't that enough?

Groups come back with: each person's figure + one strengthened purpose statement.

## Debrief Questions
1. What was the most common weakness in the proposals you heard — corpus size, public domain, or institutional stake?
2. Did any figure generate strong disagreement about suitability? What was the crux?
3. What's one thing you'd add to your purpose statement after peer feedback?

## Facilitator Notes
- Some participants will propose living figures or recently deceased figures — gently redirect toward pre-1931 corpus; this is where they'll hit copyright problems
- "Institutional stake" is the hardest criterion for many participants — push for specificity. "The library has always been interested in this" is not a stake.
- The purpose statement exercise is the most valuable thing in this session — spend extra time here if you have it
