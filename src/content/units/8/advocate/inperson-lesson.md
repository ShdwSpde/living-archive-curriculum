# Unit 8: RAG Query Pipeline — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Tables for groups of 3–4, projector for live archive demo, whiteboard for quality mapping
**Materials:** Printed query log template (one per person), live archive accessible on participant devices, sticky notes for quality rating gallery

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: "best query and worst query" — share from your prep task log |
| 0:10–0:30 | Facilitated presentation: query types + evaluation framework |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: structured testing + usage guidelines |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share: quality map + usage guidelines excerpts |

## Warm-Up Activity

**Best query and worst query (10 min)**

Each participant shares two queries from their prep task log: their best result (the one they were most impressed by) and their worst result (the one they were most disappointed by). Write both on sticky notes — green for best, red for worst — and post them on the whiteboard.

Facilitator scans the red notes: "What patterns do you see in the poor-quality queries?" (Usually: factual questions, very vague questions, or questions about topics not well-represented in the corpus.) And the green notes: "What do the high-quality queries have in common?" (Usually: specific, interpretive, focused.)

This observation — patterns in what works and what doesn't — is the foundation of good usage guidelines.

## Facilitated Presentation

**Query types + evaluation framework (20 min)**

**Part 1: The query quality spectrum (8 min)**

On the whiteboard, draw a 2x2 matrix: specific vs. vague on one axis, interpretive vs. factual on the other. Place example queries in each quadrant. Show where the archive performs best (specific + interpretive) and worst (factual, regardless of specificity).

Live demonstration: ask one query from each quadrant on the archive. The contrast between the specific+interpretive response and the factual response is usually stark and convincing.

**Part 2: The evaluation framework in practice (7 min)**

Walk through the three evaluation methods:
- Citation check: read the cited passage and ask whether it supports the claim
- Rephrasing test: ask the same question three ways, compare citations
- Expert test: ask a question you know the answer to

Live demo: run the expert test on Du Bois's view of double consciousness. Read the response, evaluate the citations, identify any overclaiming.

Ask the room: if you were testing your own figure's archive and you found that responses about one specific topic consistently cite the wrong works — what does that tell you? (Coverage gap: the right works aren't in the corpus, or weren't ingested correctly.)

**Part 3: Communicating quality to your team (5 min)**

Show the difference between unhelpful feedback ("the AI is wrong") and actionable feedback ("responses about the figure's views on labor consistently cite works from before 1910 and don't surface the more relevant essays from 1920–1925, which are in the corpus — this suggests those later works aren't being retrieved"). The second version is a technical diagnosis, not just a complaint.

## Workshop Activity

**Structured testing + usage guidelines (30 min)**

**Phase 1 (15 min): Structured query testing**

Using the printed query log, each participant runs and documents 8 queries:
- 2 specific + interpretive
- 2 comparative
- 2 vague (to understand the failure mode)
- 2 expert tests (questions you know the answer to)

For each query: rate quality (1–3), note the citations, and write one sentence about what the result tells you.

**Phase 2 (15 min): Usage guidelines draft**

Using the six-section framework from the async reading, draft a usage guidelines section for your figure's archive. Focus on the two sections that reflect your testing findings:
- "What questions work best" — informed by your query log
- "What the archive doesn't cover" — informed by your knowledge of the corpus

Write at an 8th-grade reading level. Avoid technical jargon. Two sentences per section maximum.

## Synthesis / Gallery Share

**Quality map + usage guidelines excerpts (15 min)**

Quality map: On the whiteboard, create a 2x2 grid (good/poor × expected/unexpected). Participants post sticky notes for their most notable query results. This creates a visual picture of the archive's strengths and gaps.

Usage guidelines: Each participant reads their "What questions work best" sentence aloud. Facilitator notes any language that is too technical, too vague, or that assumes knowledge most users won't have.

Close by naming the deliverable: each participant has a draft of two usage guidelines sections. These are real contributions to the archive's final documentation.

## Facilitator Notes
- The expert test is the most revealing evaluation method — make sure every participant attempts it, even those who consider themselves non-experts on the figure
- Some participants will find that their archive performs better than expected; others will find serious gaps. Both are valuable outcomes — the session isn't designed to celebrate or condemn any archive, just to understand it
- Usage guidelines that are written defensively ("this archive cannot guarantee accuracy") are less useful than guidelines that are written constructively ("here's how to get the most out of this archive") — coach toward the constructive framing
- The quality map gallery walk is brief — 2 minutes max — but it gives a collective picture that individual reflection doesn't
