# Unit 1: Why Living Archives — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Cluster tables (4 per table), projection screen
**Materials:** Printed "Figure Cards" (one set per table), sticky notes, markers, 1 laptop per table minimum

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: "What question can't you answer?" |
| 0:10–0:30 | Presentation: What a living archive does |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: Figure selection + framing |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share |

## Warm-Up Activity (10 min)
Each participant writes on a sticky note: *"A research question about a historical figure that I've wanted answered but couldn't easily find in existing digital collections."*

Post them on the board. Facilitator clusters them loosely (biographical, interpretive, comparative, contextual). Point out: these are exactly the questions a living archive is designed to answer.

## Facilitated Presentation (20 min)

### What a Living Archive Is
Pull up the Du Bois archive on the projection screen. Ask: "What was Du Bois's relationship to Booker T. Washington?" Show the answer. Point to:
- The specific citations (which book, which chapter)
- The synthesis across multiple texts
- The interpretive framing vs. the retrieved passage

"This is not a chatbot. It can only answer from the texts. It cites every claim. You can verify every answer."

### The Three Questions Every Funder Asks
1. **What does it cost?** → $5–50/month to run; one-time build cost is mostly developer time
2. **Who maintains it?** → A librarian or curator manages the corpus; a developer handles infrastructure
3. **Why not just use a search engine?** → Search finds documents. This synthesizes across documents. Different epistemological task.

### Why This Matters for Institutions
Living archives make scholarship more accessible. They're educational tools, research infrastructure, and public engagement vehicles simultaneously. A single archive can serve graduate students, undergraduates, journalists, and general public simultaneously.

## Workshop Activity (30 min)

### Figure Selection Exercise

Distribute "Figure Cards" — printed cards with brief bios of 8 potential archive subjects (Frederick Douglass, Ida B. Wells, Booker T. Washington, Anna Julia Cooper, Carter G. Woodson, Marcus Garvey, Zora Neale Hurston, Langston Hughes).

**Round 1 (10 min):** Each table picks their top 2 figures. Criteria:
- Scholarly significance to your institution's mission
- Volume and accessibility of public-domain texts
- Questions your community would want answered
- Existing digital collections they'd complement

**Round 2 (10 min):** For their top figure, each table drafts:
- One-sentence archive purpose statement
- Three specific questions the archive should be able to answer
- Who at your institution would use it

**Round 3 (10 min):** Tables compare their two finalists and choose one to bring to the gallery.

## Gallery Share (15 min)
Each table posts their one-sentence purpose statement + three questions on the wall.

Facilitator walks the gallery, noting patterns: Which figures keep appearing? What types of questions are most common? Are there institutional patterns?

Close by pointing out: every post-it represents a potential grant proposal.

## Facilitator Notes
- The warm-up almost always generates interpretive and comparative questions, not factual ones — use this to illustrate why keyword search falls short.
- Figure Cards should be prepared in advance: print 8 cards with name, dates, corpus size, and key themes. One set per table.
- Some participants will conflate "living archive" with "AI summarizer." Correct gently: the distinction is citability and source control.
