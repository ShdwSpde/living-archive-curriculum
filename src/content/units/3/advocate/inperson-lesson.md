# Unit 3: Sourcing the Corpus — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Tables for groups of 3–4, projector, whiteboard, wall space for posting clearance audit sheets
**Materials:** Printed copyright timeline reference (one per person), printed provenance documentation template, colored markers (green/yellow/red for timeline exercise), large paper or poster sheets for audit display

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: "one clear, one unclear" copyright status |
| 0:10–0:30 | Facilitated presentation: public domain + digitization team collaboration |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: corpus clearance audit + provenance documentation |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share: clearance tables posted on the wall |

## Warm-Up Activity

**"One clear, one unclear" (10 min)**

Each participant writes two sticky notes:
- **Green:** One text from their proposed corpus that is clearly public domain (title, year, brief reason)
- **Yellow:** One text that they're uncertain about (title, year, what's unclear)

Post both on the whiteboard. Facilitator reads through the yellows aloud and classifies each uncertainty type: "Date issue — we'd need to verify publication year," "Periodical issue — some issues of this magazine may post-date 1930," "Rights holder issue — this was published by an organization, not an individual." Naming the uncertainty type is half the work of resolving it.

## Facilitated Presentation

**Public domain + digitization team collaboration (20 min)**

**Part 1: Public domain in plain terms (8 min)**

The legal framework in accessible language: public domain is a defined legal status, not a gray area. Everything published in the U.S. before 1931 is yours to use — no permission required, no fees, no attribution obligation (though attribution is good scholarly practice). Walk through the three categories using the printed timeline handout:
- Green zone: pre-1931 — clearly usable
- Yellow zone: 1931–1977 — requires copyright research (registration records, renewal records)
- Red zone: post-1978 — almost certainly under copyright

Special cases worth naming:
- Government publications (always public domain, no date restriction)
- Anonymous works (the date-of-publication rule still applies; author's identity is irrelevant)
- Translated or edited compilations (the translation may be copyrighted even if the underlying text isn't)

**Part 2: Evaluating a scan for institutional use (6 min)**

What the Advocate needs to know about scan quality: you don't need to evaluate it technically, but you need to know what quality standard to specify when commissioning digitization and how to report quality to funders. Introduce the 1–2–3 scale and the random-sample approach (10–15% of corpus, documented in a spreadsheet). The key phrase for grant reporting: "X% of the corpus meets quality standard 2 or above as assessed by [evaluator name] on [date]."

**Part 3: Working with your institution's digitization team (6 min)**

The translation challenge: digitization teams work in file formats, DPI, and preservation standards; your archive needs clean text files. The two teams are often not speaking the same language. Walk through the five questions to ask any digitization team:
1. What file formats do you produce?
2. What DPI do you scan at?
3. Do you have an OCR quality review process?
4. What metadata do you capture?
5. What are your turnaround timelines?

And the three things to offer in return: clear specifications, technical context, a feedback loop.

## Workshop Activity

**Corpus clearance audit + provenance documentation (30 min)**

**Phase 1 (15 min): Clearance audit**

Using the printed template, each participant lists 10 texts from their proposed corpus and classifies each:
- Green (clearly public domain): publication year confirmed pre-1931, or government publication
- Yellow (requires research): year is 1931–1977, or year is unknown, or periodical with mixed dates
- Red (likely under copyright): post-1978 or confirmed later

For each yellow text, write one sentence: "To clear this text, I need to find out: ___."

**Phase 2 (15 min): Provenance documentation**

Using the provenance template, document 3 texts from your corpus in full: title, author, publication year and place, source (Internet Archive URL or library name), date accessed, quality notes. This is not a test — the point is to practice the format. These 3 provenance records are a real deliverable you can bring to your institution.

## Synthesis / Gallery Share

**Clearance tables on the wall (15 min)**

Each group posts their clearance audit table on the wall. 5-minute silent gallery walk: read the yellow and red entries on other groups' tables. Notice: what types of uncertainty come up most often? Are there any red-zone texts that multiple groups proposed?

10-minute discussion: What's the most common source of copyright uncertainty in this room? What's the standard operating procedure when a text is yellow — who at your institution makes the final call? Who is the right person to consult?

## Facilitator Notes
- Some participants will be surprised that the public domain is this straightforward; others will be more uncertain and want to treat every pre-1931 text as requiring review. Validate the caution but gently correct the unnecessary conservatism
- The provenance documentation exercise often reveals that participants have incomplete information about texts they've already decided to include — this is a feature, not a problem; it surfaces gaps early
- If participants work at institutions with a copyright librarian, encourage them to identify that person and invite them to a future session
- The gallery walk works best if groups actually read each other's tables — prompt them to look for yellow entries on tables other than their own and ask: "Have they identified the right question to answer?"
