# Unit 3: Sourcing the Corpus — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Slides, shared provenance documentation template, copyright timeline reference sheet

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: what's one copyright or provenance question you brought from the prep task? |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: public domain in practice + working with digitization teams |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: corpus clearance audit for your figure |
| 1:00–1:15 | Debrief: common clearance decisions and how to communicate them |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: The Public Domain Is Not Optional**
- The archive's legal foundation is the public domain, not fair use
- The 1931 threshold as of 2026 — and why it advances annually
- Three categories: clearly public domain / requires research / almost certainly under copyright
- Government publications: always safe, no date restriction
- The key message: clarity now prevents legal exposure later

**Slide 2: The Periodical Problem**
- Each issue of a periodical must be evaluated individually — not the run as a whole
- Show example: a figure's editorials in a magazine published 1910–1945
- Color-code the timeline: green before 1931, yellow 1931–1938, red after
- Practical rule: if any issue in your download batch might post-date 1930, check every issue
- What to do with ambiguous dates: consult your library's copyright librarian

**Slide 3: Working with a Digitization Team**
- What they do well: high-quality scans, metadata standards, preservation infrastructure
- What they don't know you need: clean text files, not just images; specific quality thresholds; speed
- Questions to ask: file formats, DPI, OCR quality review process, turnaround timelines
- What to offer: clear specifications, technical context, feedback loop
- The "translation" problem: you need to speak their language and explain your needs in theirs

**Slide 4: Evaluating Scan Quality for Grant Reporting**
- The 10–15% random sample method — why random, why that percentage
- The 1–2–3 rating scale: poor / acceptable / clean
- The reportable metric: "X% of corpus at quality 2 or above"
- What funders typically ask for: percentage meeting standard, date of evaluation, evaluator name
- Keep the evaluation spreadsheet — you will need it for the final report

**Slide 5: Provenance as Curatorial Trust**
- Scholars will ask: where did this come from? Can I cite it?
- The six provenance fields: title, author, year, place, source URL, access date
- Where to store provenance: not in the code repository — in a curatorial record
- The difference between provenance (where it came from) and quality (how good it is)
- Both matter; neither substitutes for the other

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 3–4**

Each group works through a copyright clearance audit for one participant's proposed corpus. Using the shared template:

1. List 10 texts from the proposed corpus (5 min)
2. For each, classify: clearly public domain / requires research / unclear (5 min)
3. For any that "require research": write down exactly what you need to find out and where you would look (10 min)
4. Identify any texts you would exclude immediately — why? (5 min)

Groups come back with: a clearance status table for 10 texts and a list of any texts requiring further research.

## Debrief Questions
1. How many texts in your groups required further copyright research? What was the most common reason for uncertainty?
2. Did any group decide to exclude a text? What was the threshold for that decision?
3. How would you explain the copyright clearance process to a non-librarian at your institution — a department chair, a grant officer, a community partner?

## Facilitator Notes
- Some participants will be overly conservative about copyright ("we should get permission for everything just to be safe") — this is understandable but impractical; help them see that the public domain is a genuine legal protection, not a shortcut
- The periodical problem tends to generate the most confusion — walk through a concrete example slowly, issue by issue
- "Clearance" language resonates with participants from publishing or legal backgrounds; "public domain" language resonates with librarians; use both
- If time is short, compress Slide 3 (digitization team) — it's less urgent for Advocates without active digitization partnerships
