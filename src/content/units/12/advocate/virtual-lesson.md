# Unit 12: Scaling to Community — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Slides, community partnership pitch drafts (prepared in async), examples of existing HBCU digital humanities projects

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: share your community partnership pitch — who did you identify? |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: community-centered archives and consortium models |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: replication grant planning |
| 1:00–1:15 | Full group debrief + stewardship role discussion |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: Who Owns the Archive's Meaning?**
- Technical ownership (the code, the deployment): the developer and the institution
- Interpretive ownership: contested — the institution, the scholar community, the descendant community
- The advocate's role: broker between these groups, ensure all have voice
- Frame: a living archive that the community it represents doesn't recognize as legitimate has failed, regardless of its technical quality

**Slide 2: Two Community Partnership Models**
- Descendant advisory model: community evaluates framing and representation, not technology
- Corpus contribution model: community contributes materials outside the public-domain canon
- Requirements for each: timeline, attribution, corrections process
- Common failure mode: consulting community after the build instead of before

**Slide 3: The AI Synthesis Ethics Conversation**
- The three questions every advocate must be able to answer (from the reading)
- Frame: "This is a synthesis tool, not a speaking simulation"
- The temporal flattening problem: how to communicate that the archive doesn't capture intellectual evolution
- Practical safeguard: a prominent disclaimer on the chat interface, updated language in the system prompt

**Slide 4: HBCU and Library Consortium Models**
- Why these institutional settings are natural homes for living archives
- HBCU model: shared infrastructure, corpus expertise distributed across institutions
- Library consortium model: builds on existing digitization investment
- Show examples: existing HBCU digital humanities centers and their current scope

**Slide 5: The Archive Stewardship Role**
- "Archive steward" as an ongoing institutional position, not a project role
- Responsibilities: corpus maintenance, quality monitoring, community liaison, grant renewal
- Funding: 0.25–0.5 FTE appropriate for a mature archive
- Making the case to an institution: stewardship is infrastructure, not overhead
- The replication clause: how one archive justifies investment in a role that can maintain many

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 3–4**

Each group works on a replication grant planning exercise. Using a target figure (assigned or chosen), draft:

1. The replication clause (one paragraph, from the async reading model)
2. The community partnership section of the proposal (who you'd involve, at what stage, with what role)
3. The stewardship section (what institutional commitment you'd need, what the FTE looks like)
4. The Year 3 sustainability plan (endowment, consortium, or subsidy — be specific)

Groups report back with their replication clause and one sentence on what makes their consortium model work.

## Debrief Questions
1. What made the community partnership section hardest to write in concrete terms? What information do you still need?
2. The "archive stewardship" role is relatively new in institutional vocabulary. How do you make the case for this position to an academic dean who is used to thinking in terms of research projects with end dates?
3. You've built one archive. A colleague at another institution wants to build one too. What's the first thing you send them?

## Facilitator Notes
- The "who owns the archive's meaning?" framing on Slide 1 generates the richest discussion but also the most discomfort. Some participants from research-intensive institutions will feel that scholarly independence is at stake. The important clarification: community partnership is about framing and representation, not about scholarly claims. Communities don't get veto power over historical scholarship; they do get voice in how their figure is represented.
- The AI synthesis ethics discussion can get abstract quickly. Keep it grounded with the specific example of temporal flattening — Du Bois's views on emigration changed dramatically. Does the archive handle this correctly? This is a verifiable question, not a philosophical debate.
- The archive stewardship role section often surprises participants by framing a "tech support" function as a meaningful scholarly and community role. Lean into this: the steward is the person who ensures the archive remains a living artifact rather than a static one.
- The replication clause breakout works best when groups name a specific second institution (not hypothetical). Ask them to name a real HBCU or library consortium partner. Specificity makes the exercise valuable.
