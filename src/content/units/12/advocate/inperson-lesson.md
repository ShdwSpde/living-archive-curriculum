# Unit 12: Scaling to Community — In-Person Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Room setup:** Cluster tables (4 per table), projection screen, whiteboard, large paper for tables
**Materials:** Printed "Community Partnership Scenarios" cards, replication grant worksheet, stewardship role description template

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Warm-up: the community consult question |
| 0:10–0:30 | Presentation: community-centered archives and the stewardship model |
| 0:30–1:00 | Workshop: replication grant and stewardship role design |
| 1:00–1:15 | Gallery share + closing reflection |

## Warm-Up Activity (10 min)
Post this scenario on the board:

> A major research university is launching a living archive of Ida B. Wells. The technical team spent six months building it. Two weeks before launch, a descendant of Wells's brother contacts the project director and says: "My family has never been consulted about this. Who gave you the right to do this?"

Ask the room: What do you do? Is it too late to bring in community partners? What should have happened differently?

Take responses. Don't resolve the scenario completely — the goal is to surface the tension between project momentum and community accountability, which the presentation will address.

## Facilitated Presentation (20 min)

### Why Community Partnership Comes First

Revisit the warm-up scenario. The answer to "is it too late?" depends on what "community partnership" means.

If it means: "getting permission to use the figure's public-domain writings" — it's never required, and the question is moot.

If it means: "ensuring the archive represents the figure in a way the community recognizes as legitimate" — then two weeks before launch is too late.

The distinction matters because the first kind of consultation is about legal access (rarely an issue with public-domain figures); the second is about interpretive legitimacy (always relevant).

Draw on the whiteboard: a timeline with "Community consultation" at four possible points:
1. Before the build starts (ideal)
2. During the build (good)
3. During soft launch (acceptable)
4. After public launch (reactive — prevents future trust)

### The Stewardship Role

The archive stewardship role is often missing from grant proposals because institutions think of digital projects as having endpoints. They don't. An archive that isn't maintained becomes an embarrassment — broken links, outdated software, degraded AI responses, no process for corrections.

Describe the steward's responsibilities:
- **Corpus maintenance:** adding new scholarly editions, removing corrupted texts
- **Quality monitoring:** running the test suite quarterly, reviewing user-reported issues
- **Community liaison:** communicating with community partners about framing questions
- **Grant renewal:** writing the Year 3+ funding proposal before Year 2 ends

This is 0.25–0.5 FTE. Not full-time. Not a volunteer job. An institutional appointment with budget backing.

On the whiteboard, write: "One archive steward can maintain 3–4 archives simultaneously — if the methodology is documented and the infrastructure is standardized." This is the argument for the replication model: investment in one good steward compounds across multiple archives.

### The Replication Argument

Show the replication clause from the reading. Explain: this transforms a $150,000 single-project grant into an argument for $400,000 infrastructure investment. The funder gets three archives for the price of one, plus a documented methodology that any institution can use.

The key elements of a credible replication claim:
1. The methodology is actually documented (not just "we'll write a guide someday")
2. At least one specific partner institution is named
3. The documentation is maintained by the steward role, not an afterthought

## Workshop Activity (30 min)

### Round 1: Community Partnership Scenario Cards (10 min)
Each table receives a Community Partnership Scenario card with a specific situation:
- "Your institution is building an archive of Carter G. Woodson. The Association for the Study of African American Life and History (ASALH) has existing archives. What do you ask them for, and what do you offer?"
- "Your archive of Anna Julia Cooper is nearly complete. A faculty member at an HBCU where Cooper taught wants to review the framing. The faculty member is not a historian. What role do they have?"
- "A family descendant of Frederick Douglass wants to contribute a private family document. How do you handle provenance, attribution, and the decision about whether to include it?"

Tables work through their scenario and develop a two-minute briefing: what's the community partnership model, what are the commitments, what's the timeline?

### Round 2: Replication Grant Design (20 min)
Each table uses the replication grant worksheet to design a multi-institution living archives initiative:
1. Lead institution (which institution hosts infrastructure and steward?)
2. Partner institutions (which 2–3 institutions contribute corpus expertise?)
3. Target figures (which three figures across partner institutions)
4. The replication clause (write the paragraph)
5. The stewardship commitment (what institutional position, what FTE, which budget line?)

Tables fill the large paper on their table. This becomes the gallery.

## Gallery Share (15 min)
Tables post their large papers on the wall. Free gallery walk for 7 minutes — participants read other tables' proposals.

Facilitator reconvenes the group and asks:
- Which lead institution arrangement appeared most often? Why?
- Which community partnership model seemed most feasible?
- What would you steal from another table's design for your own proposal?

Close with: "Every paper on this wall is a viable grant proposal structure. You've just spent 30 minutes doing the kind of collaborative thinking that usually takes three months of committee work."

## Facilitator Notes
- The warm-up scenario is intentionally uncomfortable. Some participants may defend the university in the scenario; others will side with the family. The goal is not to produce the "right" answer but to surface the assumptions each participant brings to community consultation.
- The stewardship role discussion resonates strongly with librarians and weakly with faculty — faculty are accustomed to thinking in project terms, librarians in service terms. Adjust your framing based on the room composition.
- Scenario cards should be prepared in advance with enough specificity to generate real discussion. Vague scenarios ("an archive of a historical figure") produce generic discussions. Named figures, named organizations, named institutional constraints produce useful ones.
- The gallery walk is most productive if participants are encouraged to read every table's paper, not just the ones from their social network. Explicitly ask people to move to a table they haven't interacted with.
