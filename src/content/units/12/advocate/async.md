# Unit 12: Scaling to Community — Async Materials
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 30–45 min

## Learning Objectives
- Articulate at least two models for involving descendant communities in corpus curation and describe the ethical obligations each model creates
- Identify the institutional conditions that make HBCU and library consortium replication viable
- Draft a "replication clause" for a grant proposal that invites other institutions to build archives using the same methodology

## Reading

### Community Partnership in Corpus Curation

A living archive is not just a technical artifact — it's a curatorial interpretation. Decisions about which texts to include, how to frame figures, which community voices appear in the attribution, and what questions the archive is designed to answer all carry ethical weight. This is especially true for archives of Black historical figures, where institutional history has often meant exclusion, misrepresentation, or appropriation of community knowledge.

Two models for community partnership deserve serious consideration before launch:

**Descendant community advisory model:** Identify organizations or individuals with direct community connections to the figure — family descendants, historically connected churches or schools, established community organizations that hold the figure's memory. Invite them into an advisory relationship before launch, not after. Their role is not to approve the technology but to evaluate the framing: "Does this archive represent [Figure] in a way that honors how our community understands him/her/them?"

This model requires lead time (months, not weeks), genuine listening, and willingness to change course based on feedback. It also produces a community of advocates who will promote the archive more credibly than any institutional press release.

**Corpus contribution model:** Invite community partners to contribute materials not available in public-domain digital archives — family documents, oral histories, church records, community newspapers. This expands the corpus beyond what institutional digitization has made available and directly involves communities in the archive's construction.

This model requires robust provenance documentation, clear agreements about credit and attribution, and decisions about how to handle materials that are sensitive or contested.

### The Ethics of AI Synthesis of Historical Figures

A living archive creates a new kind of artifact: an AI-generated synthesis that speaks in the mode of a historical figure's ideas. This is not the same as the figure speaking, and the distinction matters ethically.

Questions every advocate should be able to answer:

**Does the archive allow users to confuse AI synthesis with primary sources?**
The answer should be no: every response should be clearly framed as synthesis, every claim cited to specific texts, and the UI should never use first-person voice ("I believe") to describe the figure's positions.

**Who decides when the archive gets something wrong about how the figure is understood in their community?**
This question requires a named process, not just a general commitment to accuracy. A corrections process — how users report errors, who reviews them, how quickly corrections are made — should exist before launch.

**What happens when AI synthesis flattens the evolution of a figure's thinking?**
Du Bois's positions on emigration, Pan-Africanism, and the Soviet Union changed significantly across his life. An AI system that synthesizes across his full corpus without temporal context can produce a unified "Du Bois position" that doesn't reflect his actual intellectual history. Advocates need to communicate this limitation explicitly.

### HBCU and Library Consortium Models

Living archives have natural institutional homes in HBCUs and in library consortia. These settings offer both infrastructure advantages and mission alignment:

**HBCU model:** Historically Black Colleges and Universities often have deep archival holdings related to the figures most likely to become living archives, as well as direct community connections. A consortium of three or four HBCUs — each contributing corpus curation and technical maintenance — could sustain a network of living archives more durably than any single institution.

Practical arrangement: one HBCU hosts the infrastructure, others contribute corpus expertise and faculty engagement. Grant funding covers Year 1 build and one year of operations; consortium contributions cover Year 2+.

**Library consortium model:** Academic library consortia (e.g., HathiTrust, LYRASIS, regional consortia) have existing infrastructure for shared digital collections. A living archive could be positioned as a value-added layer on top of existing digitized collections — the consortium provides the corpus, a lead institution provides the AI infrastructure.

### Writing the Replication Clause

A well-written grant proposal invites replication explicitly. The replication clause signals to funders that this is infrastructure investment, not a one-off project:

> "This project will produce not only the [Figure] Living Archive but a documented, open-source methodology for building living archives of other historical figures. The methodology will include corpus preparation guidelines, technical infrastructure documentation, and an implementation guide suitable for institutions without dedicated digital humanities staff. We anticipate that [N] institutions will replicate this methodology within 36 months of launch."

The replication clause makes three things clear: the methodology is transferable, the documentation will be maintained, and you're tracking uptake. This converts a single project into an infrastructure investment in the funder's eyes.

3. What would the replication clause look like for a grant proposal you're considering? Write the paragraph.
