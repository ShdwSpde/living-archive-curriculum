# Unit 9: Pitfalls & Quality Control — Virtual Lesson
**Pathway:** Archive Advocate
**Duration:** 75 min
**Materials:** Live archive access, slides, shared quality audit template

## Agenda
| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:10 | Check-in: share your test set results — any surprises? |
| 0:10–0:35 | Presentation: recognizing quality problems + stakeholder communication |
| 0:35–1:00 | Breakout: complete a 20-question quality audit together |
| 1:00–1:15 | Debrief: quality distribution across the group + audit report drafts |

## Slide Outline

**Slide 1: What Advocates Can See That Developers Can't**
- Domain expertise: you know what the figure actually wrote; you can recognize wrong claims
- Stakeholder perspective: you use the archive the way users use it — not through test scripts
- Curatorial judgment: you know which omissions are significant vs. acceptable
- Pattern recognition over time: you notice when quality changes between sessions
- The Advocate's quality role is not secondary to the Developer's — it's complementary

**Slide 2: The Three Quality Failure Modes**
- Bad answers: factually wrong, overclaiming, or unsupported by citations
- Wrong citations: the right topic, the wrong source — points to retrieval problems
- Inconsistency: same question, different answers — points to corpus or threshold issues
- For each: how to recognize it, how to document it, what to tell the technical team
- Show real examples of each from a live archive session

**Slide 3: Setting Stakeholder Expectations**
- The pre-launch briefing: what the archive covers, what it's best at, what it can't do
- The most common overclaims: "it knows everything," "it's like talking to [figure]," "it replaces the library"
- The honest framing: "a research tool that helps explore [figure]'s writings"
- What to do when a user reports a quality problem: write it down, forward it specifically, follow up
- The trust-building cycle: quality problem → acknowledged → investigated → fixed → communicated

**Slide 4: The Quality Audit Process**
- 20–30 test questions, built deliberately to cover: well-covered topics, thin coverage, factual, comparative
- Run at each version cycle: after ingestion updates, after prompt changes, after threshold adjustments
- Rate each response: strong / acceptable / poor
- Track trends: is quality improving or degrading?
- The audit is a process, not a one-time check — it provides longitudinal quality data

**Slide 5: Writing an Actionable Quality Report**
- The unhelpful report: "the AI gives wrong answers sometimes"
- The useful report: topic + failure pattern + test evidence + hypotheses
- Structure: date/version, quality distribution, new failure modes, degraded queries, recommendations
- Who reads it: the technical team (to fix things) and institutional stakeholders (to track progress)
- File it with the project documentation, not just in someone's inbox

## Breakout Activity (25 min)
**Groups of 4, collaborative audit**

Each group builds and runs a 20-question audit together using the shared quality audit template:
1. Build the 20-question set collaboratively (each person contributes 5 questions) (5 min)
2. Divide: each person runs their 5 questions and rates the responses (10 min)
3. Compile results into the template: quality distribution, notable poor responses (5 min)
4. Write one paragraph of quality audit report prose (5 min)

Groups come back with: a filled quality audit template and a draft audit paragraph.

## Debrief Questions
1. What's the quality distribution across all groups — what percentage "strong," "acceptable," "poor"? What does that tell you about the archive's overall state?
2. Did any group find a consistent failure pattern (e.g., poor performance on a specific topic or query type)?
3. If you had to prioritize one type of quality problem for the technical team to investigate first, what would it be?

## Facilitator Notes
- The "3 quality failure modes" slide requires real examples to be compelling — prepare 2–3 examples from actual live archive sessions, not fabricated scenarios
- The pre-launch stakeholder communication framing is important: Advocates who brief stakeholders well before launch have much more productive conversations when quality issues arise
- The collaborative audit in the breakout sometimes produces inconsistent ratings across the group — this is a feature, not a problem; discuss what drives the inconsistency (different interpretations of "strong" vs. "acceptable," different levels of domain expertise)
- The audit template should be available as a shareable document that participants can take home and adapt for their own projects
